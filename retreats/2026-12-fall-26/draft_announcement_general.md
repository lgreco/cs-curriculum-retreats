Please **save the date** for the Fall 2026 Curriculum Retreat. We'll meet, on campus (this is an on-site only meeting), on **Wednesday, December 9** from 9 AM to 3 PM to discuss instructional operations and strategy. All teaching members of the department are welcome at every session. 

This tenth retreat comes at a pivotal moment, and I want to be direct about why.

AI is changing what it means to practice, and therefore to teach, computing. It is doing so just as other pressures are stacking up: declining numbers of college-age students, significant cuts in research funding, and reduced interest from international students in light of immigration policy. Any one of these would be a serious challenge. Together, they put at stake not only computer science as a discipline but academia as an enterprise. Our department's survival as an independent unit depends on a coherent strategy.

That stake is why we need to take on an existential question: what our programs should become as AI reshapes the discipline and the professions we prepare students for. We are a small department, and our capacity is limited. Therefore we must be deliberate about where we invest our effort. As we try to reinvent ourselves, the work is to decide, together, which content has outlived its relevance and which new content shall replace it, so that our programs remain worth choosing.

We're hardly alone in this search. Countless CS departments face the same uncertainty about the future of their programs as we do, and they too are trying to secure a safe place for them in the future ahead.

The success of this retreat is really up to all of us. Whether it's a reflection, a topic for the agenda, or a reading to add to the list, your contribution is what will make the conversation worth having.

In the absence of silver bullets, we'll go back to the fundamentals: how can we make a difference in the classroom by caring for our students, imparting valuable soft and like skills, and a foundational understanding of the discipline.

That return to fundamentals is also why our retreats have traditionally centered on the foundational sequence (COMP 141, 163, 170, 264, 271, 272, and 363). We'll begin there again, and this time we'll also ask how changes in that sequence affect the rest of the curriculum.

The single most important thing you can do to prepare is to write a short reflection, using some or all of the following five reflection areas. This retreat is about deciding what we retire and what we introduce, and those decisions can only be as good as the classroom experience we bring to them. Every perspective matters, including from those who can only join for part of the day. Please make the time for it. Candid, half-formed thoughts are exactly what will make the conversation productive.

1. **Teaching practice**
   - How has AI changed the way you run your classroom: lectures, labs, office hours, communication with students?
   - Do you have a stated AI policy for your courses? What has worked, and what hasn't?
   - Have you used AI yourself to prepare materials, give feedback, or support students? What did you learn?
2. **Assessment and integrity**
   - How has AI changed the way you evaluate student work and engagement?
   - Which of your assessments still measure what you intend them to, and which no longer do?
   - Have you tried alternative formats (oral exams, in-class work, process logs, code walkthroughs, version-history review)? With what results?
3. **Content and learning outcomes**
   - Have you changed the deliverables or emphasis of your courses in response to AI developments in your field? How?
   - What must students still be able to do without AI by the end of your course, and why?
   - What has become more important to teach because of AI (reading and evaluating code, specification, testing, debugging, judgment about tool output)?
   - Do your courses, as currently planned, prepare students for an AI-mediated workplace? What evidence do you have, from employers, alumni, or students?
4. **The curriculum as a whole**
   - For the foundational sequence (COMP 141, 163, 170, 264, 271, 272, 363): where should topics be added, compressed, reordered, or retired?
   - What changes do you want to see in the foundational sequence that will make your downstream courses more productive? (This is a question I ask at every retreat.)
   - Are we consistent enough across sections and courses (AI policies, expectations, tools) that students experience a coherent program?
   - Do all students have equitable access to the AI tools our courses assume? Should the department provide or standardize tools?
   - What support would you need (time, training, tools, etc.) to make the changes you think are necessary?
5. **Blue sky**
   - If we were free of operational legacies and administrative constraints, how would you redesign the curriculum to best serve our students?

To give myself time to pull these together into a brief for everyone, I'd propose sending me your reflection by Monday, December 7. Even a few sentences will help.
