## Readings

* [Sakellaridis, Y. (2026, October 21). *Paralysis.* Proofs and Prompts.](https://proofsandprompts.com/2026/10/21/artificial-intelligence-and-the-mathematical-community/)

  Sakellaridis, a Johns Hopkins mathematician, argues that AI companies’ push to recoup massive investment drives them to capture and own scientific output: they train on publicly funded research without attribution, operate opaquely, and threaten to displace human mathematical understanding. He faults professional organizations, universities, prominent mathematicians, and policymakers for a collective paralysis that leaves this unchecked and may push a generation of aspiring scientists away from mathematics. This is relevant because his argument is extensible to other STEM fields, including computing.

* [Sparrow, R., & Flenady, G. (2025). *Bullshit universities: The future of automated education.* AI & Society, 40, 5285–5296](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02340-8) (Open Access paper).

  Sparrow and Flenady argue against using AI in higher education. Drawing on [Frankfurt’s account of bullshit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Bullshit) and Brandom’s [inferentialist semantics](https://sites.pitt.edu/~rbrandom/Courses/2022%20Phil%20of%20Language/Reasons%20texts/Brandom_-_Articulating_Reasons.pdf), they argue that generative AI output is indifferent to truth and, because machines cannot act or be held responsible, strictly meaningless. They also predict that “AI assistants” will lead to replacing teachers through automation bias and cost pressures. Such replacement would erode the learning-how, role modeling, and social recognition that education depends on. They recommend confining AI instruction to dedicated study-skills units and investing instead in smaller classes, passionate human teachers, and more oral and invigilated assessment.

* Hasanah, N. U., Aulia, A. A., Abdillah, M. A., & Poedjiono, N. D. (2026). [Artificial intelligence in education: Can AI improve teacher performance or will it replace them?](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/409299873_Artificial_Intelligence_in_Education_Can_AI_Improve_Teacher_Performance_or_Will_It_Replace_Them). Didakta: International Journal of Social Sciences, Language, and Education, 1(3), 21–31.

  This literature review is a relatively short read that concludes that AI is unlikely to replace teachers entirely but will reshape their professional roles through collaborative human-AI models. *(Offered by Bob Yacobellis)*

* Sabani, A., Farah, M. H., Catyanadika, P. E., Dewi, D. R. S., & Tawani, V. (2026). [Rewriting the curriculum: A systematic review of generative AI-driven pedagogical change and emerging systems of learning in higher education](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2026.100667). Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, 11, 100667.

  Combining bibliometric analysis, 36 studies, and ten educator interviews, this paper argues that generative AI demands systemic curricular transformation rather than incremental instructional improvements. It proposes a five-domain framework encompassing curriculum redesign, teaching, capabilities, governance, and institutional integration. Its methodological triangulation strengthens the analysis, although the limited interview sample and narrowly selected literature constrain generalizability. The framework remains conceptual, without demonstrated effectiveness or validation across institutions.  *(Offered by Bob Yacobellis)*
