## Readings

* [Sakellaridis, Y. (2026, October 21). *Paralysis.* Proofs and Prompts.](https://proofsandprompts.com/2026/10/21/artificial-intelligence-and-the-mathematical-community/) (blog post)

  Sakellaridis, a Johns Hopkins mathematician, argues that AI companies’ push to recoup massive investment drives them to capture and own scientific output: they train on publicly funded research without attribution, operate opaquely, and threaten to displace human mathematical understanding. He faults professional organizations, universities, prominent mathematicians, and policymakers for a collective paralysis that leaves this unchecked and may push a generation of aspiring scientists away from mathematics. This is relevant because his argument is extensible to other STEM fields, including computing. *(Offered by Leo Irakliotis)*

* [Sparrow, R., & Flenady, G. (2025). *Bullshit universities: The future of automated education.* AI & Society, 40, 5285–5296](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02340-8) (Open Access paper).

  Sparrow and Flenady argue against using AI in higher education. Drawing on [Frankfurt’s account of bullshit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Bullshit) and Brandom’s [inferentialist semantics](https://sites.pitt.edu/~rbrandom/Courses/2022%20Phil%20of%20Language/Reasons%20texts/Brandom_-_Articulating_Reasons.pdf), they argue that generative AI output is indifferent to truth and, because machines cannot act or be held responsible, strictly meaningless. They also predict that “AI assistants” will lead to replacing teachers through automation bias and cost pressures. Such replacement would erode the learning-how, role modeling, and social recognition that education depends on. They recommend confining AI instruction to dedicated study-skills units and investing instead in smaller classes, passionate human teachers, and more oral and invigilated assessment.  *(Offered by Leo Irakliotis)*

* Anthropic, PBC. (2026, October 26). [Registration statement (Form S-1)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com). U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. 

  Anthropic’s [S-1 filing (October 26, 2026)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm) signals a fundamental transformation of knowledge work, with profound implications for higher education. Claude is evolving from an assistant into an autonomous collaborator capable of conducting research, developing software, analyzing information, and executing complex workflows. Anthropic also describes using Claude to accelerate the development of subsequent AI models, suggesting a potentially self-reinforcing cycle of technological improvement.

  For universities, the opportunities include personalized instruction, accelerated scientific discovery, research automation, and substantially greater faculty productivity. The risks extend beyond academic integrity to the relevance of educational programs, the economic value of degrees, institutional dependence on commercial AI providers, and the security of increasingly autonomous systems.

  Computer science faces particularly acute challenges. Claude Code already performs substantial portions of the software development lifecycle, calling into question curricula organized primarily around programming proficiency. As routine development becomes increasingly automated, entry-level employment opportunities may contract, undermining a traditional justification for studying computer science.

  The educational response cannot simply be adding AI courses. It requires reconsidering what computer scientists should know, emphasizing computational foundations, system architecture, verification, security, and the capacity to evaluate and direct increasingly capable autonomous systems. *(Offered by Leo Irakliotis. Implications for academic programs and employment are Leo's extrapolations, not forecasts established by the filing)*

* Hasanah, N. U., Aulia, A. A., Abdillah, M. A., & Poedjiono, N. D. (2026). [Artificial intelligence in education: Can AI improve teacher performance or will it replace them?](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/409299873_Artificial_Intelligence_in_Education_Can_AI_Improve_Teacher_Performance_or_Will_It_Replace_Them) Didakta: International Journal of Social Sciences, Language, and Education, 1(3), 21–31.

  This literature review is a relatively short read that concludes that AI is unlikely to replace teachers entirely but will reshape their professional roles through collaborative human-AI models. *(Offered by Bob Yacobellis)*

* Sabani, A., Farah, M. H., Catyanadika, P. E., Dewi, D. R. S., & Tawani, V. (2026). [Rewriting the curriculum: A systematic review of generative AI-driven pedagogical change and emerging systems of learning in higher education](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2026.100667). Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, 11, 100667.

  This is a more comprehensive look at how curricula should be redesigned in the context of AI. It's a long read, and I suggest reading primarily the Abstract and Introduction, and then Sections 4 & 5.  *(Offered by Bob Yacobellis)*

* Lovett, N. (2026). [The tragedy of the cognitive commons: How AI could disrupt the regeneration of professional expertise](https://doi.org/10.1177/15344843261470602). Human Resource Development Review. Advance online publication. 

Lovett argues that widespread AI adoption threatens the collective regeneration of professional expertise. As organizations automate entry-level cognitive work, they eliminate opportunities through which novices develop the deep knowledge necessary to evaluate AI outputs. This creates a “tragedy of the cognitive commons”: individually rational efficiency gains collectively undermine the expertise upon which AI systems depend. Particularly relevant to computer science education, the paper challenges institutions to preserve pathways toward independent mastery rather than merely teaching students to operate AI tools. *(Offered by Leo Irakliotis)*
